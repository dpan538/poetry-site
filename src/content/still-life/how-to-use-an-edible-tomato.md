(1)

When a tomato sits in a basket, it rarely looks like a food that needs explaining.

It is red in a direct way, its skin smooth, as if the kitchen had taken it in. People pass by and assume it will only be cut, cooked, and set on a plate. But the way a tomato truly enters a life, or enters a meal, does not begin with the knife.

It begins with judgment. A ripe tomato gives a little under the thumb. Its color is even, the stem shows no watery rot, and up close there is a faint sour scent of the plant. This is not the neutral aroma of hand brewed coffee. It is a slightly guarded greenness, mixed with diluted vinegar.

A tomato that is too hard can keep waiting at room temperature. One that is too soft should not be put off. It is already at the edge where the juice collapses, and is best used that day.

(2)

Washing a tomato need not be as complicated as handling other round things that carry seeds or secretions. Rinse the dust from its surface under running water, then rub the skin lightly. Stay a moment longer around the stem, because fine sand hides there most easily. If you eat it raw, wash first and cut after, so no dirt follows the blade into the flesh. It can be sliced thin and laid beside bread, cheese, eggs. It can be cut small and mixed with olive oil, salt, black pepper, onion, and herbs, into a bright cold dish. The sourness lifts the oil, the sweetness presses the salt down, like two hands smoothing the folds of one shirt. Not too warm, not too hard, in the gap before the water floods the cheese or lets it bleed.

(3)

Cooked, the tomato is more like a square of text opening slowly. Cut it, put it in the pan, let a little oil meet the heat first, and the skin wrinkles slightly.

Of course I should advise everyone to peel off the coat, as all mothers would, but I have no such fussiness. I let the juice come out from inside, a thin red layer forming on the bottom of the pan. Now garlic, onion, or meat can go in, and the sourness softens the heavy taste. For soup, it goes with beef brisket, egg, tofu, potato. For sauce, it needs……

Longer, until the water is gone and the flesh breaks into a thick red mud. For a finer texture, score a cross on top, scald it briefly in hot water, then move it to cold and peel. Once the skin falls away, the tomato looks quieter, treated gently by the world.

(4)

A tomato can also be kept. A fresh one should not stay long in the refrigerator, where the cold dulls the scent and turns the flesh mealy. A cut one should be sealed, chilled, and eaten soon. What cannot be finished can be cooked down to sauce, put in a clean jar, and covered with a layer of oil for a short while. Or it can be cut and frozen, for later soups and stews. A frozen tomato no longer suits a salad, because its structure has been rewritten by ice. A tomato is not water, which means it is not perfect, because a tomato is a tomato. In a hot pan, the damage becomes an advantage: the juice comes out faster, and the taste enters the other food more easily.

Using a tomato also means minding its borders. A tomato that is moldy, visibly rotten, or foul smelling should not be eaten.

Cutting away the bad part is not always reliable, since mold may have spread where no one can see. Unripe green tomatoes have their own recipes, but should not be eaten raw in quantity. People sensitive to stomach acid can eat them less on an empty stomach, or heat them, peel them, and pair them with starch or protein to soften the sting. The beauty of the tomato is not that it is always safe, but that it must be approached correctly: wash, cut, season, heat, keep, each step like carrying a red circle from the plant into the life of the tomato.
